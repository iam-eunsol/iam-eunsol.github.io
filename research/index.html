RESEARCH

When should people trust what technology tells them?

I study how much people rely on information from platforms, algorithms, and AI, what shapes that reliance, and whether it actually leads to better decisions. I start from established theories in behavioral economics and ask where they hold, and where they break, in new information environments. One stream studies static information, like video previews and algorithmic predictions; the other studies interactive advice from large language models.

Decision Complexity and Trust in Large Language Model Advisors

with João Sedoc and Arun Sundararajan

Job market paper · In preparation for submission to PNAS

As organizations increasingly adopt large language models (LLMs) for decision support, a central challenge lies in understanding when individuals are willing to trust such advisors. We theorize that decision complexity plays a critical role, as it simultaneously increases the need for guidance while reducing individuals’ ability to evaluate explanations. In a preregistered, incentive-compatible online experiment (N = 1,003) in a financial advising context, participants make investment decisions before and after receiving advice from an LLM. Complexity is manipulated through the number of lottery outcomes, and behavioral trust is measured using the weight-of-advice metric. We find that behavioral trust in LLMs increases with decision complexity, but only among individuals with higher cognitive reflection; for others, the effect attenuates due to reduced understanding of the LLM’s reasoning. Our results extend behavioral trust measures to conversational agents and highlight the alignment between decision demands and user capabilities as critical for effective human-AI collaboration.

LLMs and Consumer Bias: “Paying Not to Go to the Gym” with an LLM Advisor?

with Sagit Bar-Gill and João Sedoc

Accepted for long-paper presentation, WISE 2026 (Lisbon)

LLM advisors increasingly interact with consumers at the point of purchase, raising the question of whether they mitigate or reinforce well-documented choice biases. We study gym membership choice, where consumers systematically overestimate future attendance. In a randomized field experiment with NYU Athletics, prospective members receive information through static disclosure or interact with one of two LLM advisors given matched information. The advisor does not change plan choice at purchase, but members advised by an LLM given historical usage data overspend $17.7 more per month than those advised by a pricing-only LLM. Conversation-level analysis, an online replication (N = 687), and a digital-twin experiment show that the advisor reinforces, rather than challenges, users who are highly confident and whose estimates lie far above break-even, and that access to historical usage data increases reinforcement. The findings inform the design and regulation of consumer-facing AI advisors.

Reinforcement versus Challenge in LLM-Assisted Decision-Making

with Sagit Bar-Gill and João Sedoc

Working paper

LLM advisors may either challenge or reinforce the beliefs users bring to a decision. We develop a method for evaluating whether an LLM advisor reinforces or challenges a user’s existing belief, a construct distinct from tone, grounded in a conceptually defined rubric validated against expert human judgment and applied at scale. Focusing on settings in which consumers estimate their own future usage and tend to be biased, gym memberships and prepaid eSIM data plans, we show that the method generalizes across settings, providing a tool for evaluating whether an LLM offers a meaningful second opinion or accepts users’ assumptions without scrutiny.

Catchy or Sketchy? How Thumbnail-Title Congruence Shapes Curiosity and Video Viewership

with Jaeung Sim, Daegon Cho, and Jiyong Eom

Under review at MIS Quarterly

On video-sharing platforms, users decide what to watch based on a small set of cues jointly signaling a video’s content. We examine how the title’s semantic congruence with each thumbnail component, its image and overlaid text, shapes viewership. A field study of 10,098 YouTube videos from 233 creators reveals a modality asymmetry: image-title congruence is positively associated with subsequent views, whereas text-title congruence is negatively associated with them. Drawing on information-gap theory, we propose that congruence shapes both the perceived information gap and its expected resolvability through viewing. A preregistered experiment with 996 participants, using generative AI to manipulate both forms of congruence, supports this account: image-title congruence enhanced curiosity and video choice, whereas text-title congruence did not, and the information gap increased curiosity only when expected resolvability was high.

Redefining Objectives Under Algorithmic Ambiguity: Addressing Ambiguity Aversion through Regularization

Sole-authored

Working paper

Organizations increasingly deploy predictive models in settings where users differ in their tolerance for uncertainty about model performance. Using the α-Maxmin Expected Utility framework, I theorize that regularization induces a trade-off between the mean and the across-dataset variance of out-of-sample errors, and that ambiguity-averse users will favor objectives that temper variance even at some cost to the mean. Simulations on synthetic data show that the α-MEU objective selects different regularization levels across ambiguity attitudes, whereas in simulations seeded by a real-world meal-delivery dataset the objective becomes monotone in regularization, yielding the same choice across attitudes. The findings suggest that user-centric objective tuning can be a useful design lever and identify conditions under which ambiguity attitudes shape model preferences.
